\section{Standard DS: rotation of the circle}

Consider $T=R_{\alpha}:T^1\rightarrow T^1, x \rightarrow x+\alpha \mod 1$ where $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$ is a parameter, which is equivalently represented by $x\in[0, 1)\rightarrow \{x+\alpha\}$ where the curly braces indicate the fractional part.

This is the same as doing 0 to $2\pi$ in a circle. Notice that it's invertible with the inverse $R^{-1}_{\alpha}:T^1\rightarrow T^1,\,x\rightarrow x-\alpha$.

\begin{observation}
    $O(x_0)=\{R_{\alpha}^n(x_0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ with $R^n_{\alpha}(x_0)=x+n\alpha mod 1$ 
    Notice how this is not exactly the same as the definition, because we are truncating the integer just at the end instead of at every iteration. This may be important when dealing with computer programs, since you may want to perform the mod 1 operation just at the very end.
\end{observation}

We are now ready to make some observation about the nature of this kind of movement on the toroidal space.

\begin{proposition}
    If $\alpha=\frac{p}{q}$, $p, q\in\mathbb{Z},\, \{p, q\}=0$, all orbits are periodic with a fundamental period (i.e. the smallest of all possible periods) of $q$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    \begin{equation*}
        R_{\alpha}^n(x)=x+n\alpha \mod 1=x+n\frac{p}{q} \mod 1
    \end{equation*}
    We want to prove that $x\in Fix(R_{\alpha}^n)$ for some $n$, which is the definition of a periodic point, and the minimum $n$ for which it is true is $q$.
    So, we first show that $q$ is indeed a period:
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\alpha}^q(x)=x+q\frac{p}{q} \mod 1=x+p \mod 1=x
    \end{equation}
    because mod 1 truncates the integer, and $p\in\mathbb{Z}$. The fact that it is the minimum period is trivially determined by the fact that $\{p,q\}$.\footnote{Different from what the professor did.}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    If $\alpha\in\mathbb{I}=\{x|x\in\mathbb{R} \land x\notin\mathbb{Q}\}$\footnote{So, irrational numbers.} then the orbit $O(x)$ is dense in $T^1$ $\forall x\in T^1$
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We begin by proving that the cardinality of $O(x)=\{R_{\alpha}^n(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is $\infty$, which is demonstrated when we prove that $\alpha \in \mathbb{I}$ implies no fixed points. Proven by contradiction:
    \begin{align*}
        x\in Fix(R^n_{\alpha})\iff x+n\alpha \mod 1=x \mod 1\iff \\
        n\alpha  \mod 1 =0\iff n\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\iff\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
    \end{align*}

    Since, by hypothesis, $\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}$, then $\forall x\in T^1, x\notin Fix(R^n_\alpha)$, which, in turn, implies $\#O(x)=\infty\,\forall x\in T^1$. Since $T^1$ is compact, by Bolzano-Weirstrass's theorem we can say that $O(x)$ has an accumulation point, i.e. a point whose neighbourhoods always contain at least another point in the set. 

    So, by definition:
    \begin{equation*}
        \forall \epsilon>0 \exists m,n\in\mathbb{Z}(m>n) \text{ such that } d(R_{\alpha}^n,R_{\alpha}^m)<\epsilon
    \end{equation*}
    
    Now, if we accept as true that all rotations conserve the distance: \begin{equation*}
        d(R_{\beta}(y), R_{\beta}(x))=d(x,y)\forall\beta\in\mathbb{R}
    \end{equation*}
    we may apply such property to the aforementioned points:
    \begin{equation*}
        d(R_{-n\alpha}(R_{\alpha}^m), R_{-n\alpha}(R_{\alpha}^n))=d(R^{m-n}_{\alpha}(x), x)<\epsilon
    \end{equation*}
    
    m and n though are arbitrary, so if we call $k=m-n$ we just proved that $R^k_{\alpha}(x)$ is dense in $T^1$, which implies that the same is true for $O^1$.
\end{proof}

We can deal with a fun application of this fact, but before we need a lemma.

\begin{lemma}
    $\log_{10}2\notin\mathbb{Q}$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    RAA If the statement is false, then $\log_{10}2\in\mathbb{Q}$, which implies the following: \begin{equation*}
        \exists p,q\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }10^{p/q}=2\iff 10^p=2^q
    \end{equation*}. 
    That, though is impossible because powers of 2 never end with 0. 
    
    Alternatively, to convince us forther, we can separate $10^p=2^p5^p$, which implies $2^p5^p=2^q$, but this is only possible if p and q are 0, which does not make sense.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    If $r\in\mathbb{Z}^+$ there exists an n such that the decimal representation of $2^n$ starts with the digits of r
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof} \footnote{Last part is different from the professor's, check for accuracy.}
    Consider $R_{\alpha}: T^1\rightarrow T^1$, $\alpha=\log_{10}2\notin \mathbb{Q}$

    We now notice that $\forall n\in\mathbb{N}$ we can write (uniquely) $2^n=10^{k_n}q_n$, with $k_n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $q_n\in[1,10)$. We also notice that: \begin{equation*}
        \log_{10}(2^n)\mod 1=n\log_{10}(2)\mod 1=n\alpha=R_{\alpha}^n(0) \mod 1
    \end{equation*}
    and, at the same time: \begin{align*}
        log_{10}(2^n) \mod 1=k_n+log(q_n) \mod 1&=log(q_n) \mod 1 \\\Rightarrow \{log(q_n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}&=\{R_{\alpha}^n(0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
    \end{align*}
    Thus putting the problem on the torus. This implies, because of the last results, that $\{\log_{10}q_n\}$ is dense in $T^1$, which in turn implies that $\{q_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is dense in [1, 10), as shown in \autoref{fig:density_exp}. We may now end the proof by realizing that: \begin{align*}
        A_r&=\{y\in[1, 10)| \text{the decimal representative of y starts with the digits of r}\} \\
        &=\left[\frac{r}{10^{d-1}}, \frac{r+1}{10^{d-1}}\right),\, r\in [10^{d-1}, 10^d]
    \end{align*}
    So the numbers we are looking for are an interval for any $r$. Because of the definition of density, there are infinitely many points in $A_r\cap\{q_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ for any $r$.

\subsection{Topological dynamics}
For now on, M is a topological space.
\begin{definition}
    A topological space is a space $M$ which has a topology. A topology over $M$ is a set of subsets $\tau$, called open sets, which have the following properties: \begin{itemize}
        \item $\emptyset$ and $M$ are in $\tau$;
        \item an arbitrary (infinite or finite) union of sets in $\tau$ belong to $\tau$;
        \item The intersection of any finite number of members of $\tau$ belongs to $\tau$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

We shall now define the concept of topologically transitive.

\begin{definition}
    $(M, \phi^t)$ is called topologically transitive if $\forall U, V$ nonempty open sets there exists some $t(U, V)\in\mathbb{G}$(i.e. $\mathbb{N, Z, R}_0^+, \mathbb{R}$) such that $\phi^t(U)\cap V\neq \emptyset$. We are assuming t is in $\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}_0^+, \mathbb{Z},\mathbb{N}$.
\end{definition}

In practice, what happens is what is shown in \autoref{fig:top_transitivity}.

Notice that this is a form of chaoticity.

\begin{definition}
    A locally compact separable metric space is a metric space(i.e. a set where a distance is defined) which is locally compact, which means that the closure of every ball is compact, and separable, which means that there exists a set of open sets $\{U_k\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ that is a countable basis of the topology, i.e. $\forall U$ open $\exists k|U_k\subseteq U$. Reminder that open sets, in this context, are all sets with the property: \begin{equation*}
    \forall x\in A \exists \rho>0|B_{\rho}(x)\subseteq A
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    Let M be a locally compact separable metric space\footnote{A good space.}, and $T:M\rightarrow M$ continuous. Then,  $(M, T)$ is topologically transitive $\iff$ there exists $x\in M$ such that $O(x)$ is dense.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let's prove $\Leftarrow$.

    Let's take $\{T^n(x)\}$, which is dense by hypothesis. This implies there exists $k,m (m>k)$ such that $T^k(x)\in U, T^m(x)\in V$\footnote{Notice that we may take $m>k$ as a hypothesis because, since the orbit is dense and the space is good, it covers the whole space an infinite number of times. This implies that I can decide which index is greater without problems.}. 
    So, we may write: \begin{equation*}
        T^m(x)=T^{m-k}(T^k(x))
    \end{equation*}
    And then notice that: \begin{equation*}
        T^{m-k}(T^k(x))\in T^{m-k}(U)\cap V \Rightarrow T^{m-k}(U)\cap V\neq \emptyset
    \end{equation*}
    because $T^{m-k}(T^k(x))$ is at least one point. So, $m-k$ is the $t(U, V)$ in the definition.

    We are going to prove that there is an orbit that visits all open sets in the countable basis. 

    Take $\{U_k\}$ countable basis of the topology. Since T is topologically transitive, we know that there exists $n_0\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that $T^{n_0}U_0\cap U_1\neq \emptyset$, which implies that $U_0\cap T^{-n_0}U_1\neq \emptyset$, due to the fact that, for any function, the counter-image of an interception is the interception of the counter-images. So, if the first interception is not empty then the counter-image of the interceptions is not empty. 

    Due to the openness of said interception (which is a direct consequence of the continuity of T), we may find some $V_1$, included in its closure $\bar{V_1}$ (assume it is compact), for which: \begin{equation*}
        V_1\subset\bar{V_1}\subset U_0\cap T^{-n_0}U_1
    \end{equation*}
    The point is that the interception is not empty, and it contains more than one point. 
    
    By using again the definition of topologically transitive, we may also say that \begin{equation*}
        \exists n_1 | T^{n_1}V_1\cap U_2\neq\emptyset\Rightarrow V_1\cap T^{-n_1}U_2\neq\emptyset,\,\text{open}
    \end{equation*}
    Which, again, implies that there exists some $V_2\subset\bar{V_2}\subset V_1\cap T^{-n_1}U_2$. We may repeat this process (as shown in \autoref{fig:nested}) for the whole basis:
    \begin{equation*}
        \exists V_k\subset\bar{V_k}\subset V_{k-1}\cap T^{-n_{k-1}}U_k
    \end{equation*}
    So you can say that $V=\cap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}V_k=\cap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bar{V_k}$, where the equality stems from the fact that the basis is infinite. By a property of locally compact separable metric spaces, closed sets cannot be empty, so $V\neq\emptyset\Rightarrow\exists x\in V$.

    For every $k\in\mathbb{N},\,x\in T^{-n_{k-1}}U_k\Leftrightarrow T^{n_{k-1}}(x)\in U_k$. So O(x) intercepts all elements of the countable basis, which means that it visits all open sets. 
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    Within the above general framework, if we take a dynamical system over a phase space $(M,\phi^t)$, $\phi^t$ is called minimal if all orbits are dense.
\end{definition}

An example of an easy map is the discrete translations of the torus. Take $M=[0, 1)^d\approxeq T^d$(the torus). The discrete translations are defined as $T_{\gamma}:T^d\rightarrow T^d, \gamma\in\mathbb{R}^d,$ where $ T_{\gamma}=x+\gamma \mod \mathbb{Z}^d=T_{\gamma}(x_1,..,x_d)=(x_1+\gamma_1 \mod 1,..,x_d+\gamma_d \mod 1)$. Notice that we are now dealing with the generalization of the rotation. The whole thing is shown in \autoref{fig:torus_translation}.

\begin{definition}
    A set $A$ is called T-invariant if and only if $T^{-1}A=A$. A function f is called T-invariant if $f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\iff f=f\circ T$. This kind of functions are oftentimes also called observables. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    For a set $A$, $1_A$ is called the indicator and is defined as \begin{equation*}
    1_A(x)=\begin{cases}
                1,\, x\in A \\
                0,\, x\notin A
            \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    $A$ is invariant $\iff 1_A$ is invariant.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    $A$ invariant means that $A=T^{-1}A$, which implies that $1_A=1_{T^{-1}A}$. Now, notice that $1_A\circ T=1_A(T(x))$, which is exactly the same as saying $1_A\circ T=1_{T^{-1}A}$ because $T(x)\in A \iff x\in T^{-1}A$. Since all the passages are if and only ifs, the proposition if proven.
\end{proof}  

\begin{proposition}
    Be M a locally compact separable metric space, then \begin{align*}
        \text{(M, T) is topologically transitive} \Rightarrow & \nexists f:M\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\text{ continuous,} \\&\text{T-invariant and non-constant.}
    \end{align*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We proved that (M, T) topologically transitive implies the existence of a point $x_0$ for which the orbit $O(x_0)$ is dense. Density implies that for every $x\in M$ there exists a sequence $y_i\subset O(x_0),\, y_i=T^{m_i}x_0$ such that $y_j\rightarrow x$ for $j\rightarrow\infty$. If we assume the existence of a continuous f, we may find that $f(y_j)\rightarrow f(x)$ for $j\rightarrow\infty$. Assuming that said f is also T-invariant, you get that $f(y_j)=f(T^{m_j}(x_0))=f(x_0)$. This implies that the aforementioned limit becomes an equality, because $f(y_j)$ is constant. 
    
    Concatenating the equalities, we get that  $\forall x\in M,\, f(x)=f(x_0)$. So, $f(x)$ is constant, which confirms the thesis since there is no f that satisfies all three of the conditions.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    Be M  a locally compact separable metric space, T a homeomorfism, then \begin{equation*}
        \text{T is topologically transitive} \iff \nexists U, V\neq\emptyset\text{, open, disjoint and T-invariant.}
    \end{equation*} 
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    $\Rightarrow$ is easy. 
    
    RAA. Assume there exists $U, V$ non-empty, open, disjoint and T-invariant. If T is topologically transitive, then $T^{n}U\cap V\neq\emptyset$ for some n.
    
    Since $T^nU=U$ because they are T-invariant, so $U\cap V\neq\emptyset$, but we assumed that they were indeed disjoint.

    $\Leftarrow$ is proven, again, by contradiction. 
    
    RAA Suppose T is not topologically transitive. 
    
    So, $\exists U_0, V_0$ open, non-empty sets such that $T^nU_0\cap V_0 = \emptyset \forall n\in \mathbb{Z}$\footnote{They are also disjoint, since n=0 is included and $T^0(U)=U$.}, which is simply the negation of the definition. 
    
    We then set $U=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^nU_0; V=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{n}V_0$. U and V are obviously non-empty, they are open because of the fact that T is a homeomorphism and T-invariant as we are considering all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.
    
    But they are also disjoint: if they were not, considering the way they are built, $T^mU_0 \cap T^kV_0\neq\emptyset$ for some m and k. Considering the fact that T is a homeomorphism, this implies that $T^{m-k}U_0\cap V_0\neq \emptyset$, which would negate the very first building block of this proof. So, U and V are non-empty, open, disjoint and T-invariant, which is a contradiction to our hypothesis.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    A vector is said to be rationally independent if is linearly independent on the field of rational numbers (i.e. there does not exist a vector $(r_0,...,r_d)\in\mathbb{Q}^{d+1}\setminus \{0\}$ such that $r_01+r_1\gamma_1+...+r_d\gamma_d=0$).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    A vector $(1, \gamma_1,...,\gamma_d)$ is rationally independent $\underset{1}{\iff}$ there does not exist a vector $(r_0,...,r_d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\setminus \{0\}$ such that $r_01+r_1\gamma_1+...+r_d\gamma_d=0$ $\underset{2}{\iff} k_1\gamma_1+...\notin\mathbb{Z}$ for every combination of $k_d$\footnote{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZeOmeOhToiw}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    We shall start by proving $\underset{1}{\iff}$. 
    
    $\Rightarrow$ is trivial, since is such a vector does not exist in the set of rationals, it cannot exist in the set of integer numbers.

    $\Leftarrow$ is a bit harder but still simple. 
    
    RAA if there was indeed a rational vector $(r_0,...,r_d)\in\mathbb{Q}^{d+1}\setminus \{0\}$ such that $r_01+r_1\gamma_1+...+r_d\gamma_d=0$, then we could say $r_i=\frac{p_i}{q_i},\, p_i \in \mathbb{Z}, q_i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$ for every $i \in [0, d]$. This implies \begin{equation*}
        0=r_01+r_1\gamma_1+...+r_d\gamma_d=\frac{p_0}{q_0}+\gamma_1\frac{p_1}{q_1}+...+\gamma_d\frac{p_d}{q_d}
    \end{equation*}
    Which can be reworked a bit as: \begin{equation*}
        \frac{p_0}{q_0}+\gamma_1\frac{p_1}{q_1}+...+\gamma_d\frac{p_d}{q_d}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{d}p_i\gamma_i\prod_{j\in[0, d]\setminus\{i\}}q_j}{\prod_{n=0}^{d}q_n}
    \end{equation*}
    this means, though, since by concatenating the equalities we can say that the aforementioned sum is equal to 0, that there indeed exists an integer vector that satisfies the defining condition, namely $(r_0,...,r_d), r_i=p_i\prod_{j\in[0, d]\setminus\{i\}}q_j$, which contradicts the hypothesis.
    
    The only part left to prove is that $(r_0,...,r_d), r_i=p_i\prod_{j\in[0, d]\setminus\{i\}}q_j$ is non-zero, because the absurd only leads us to the existence of an integer vector. This is trivial because the starting vector $(r_0,...,r_d)$ was non-zero, so at least one $p_i$ is non-zero itself. Multiplying it with $\prod_{j\in[0, d]\setminus\{i\}}q_j$ cannot make it zero because all of $q_j$s are non-zero by hypothesis.

    Now, we must prove $\underset{2}{\iff}$.

    $\Rightarrow$ is done by contradiction. RAA if there existed some combination of $k_d$s such that $\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_i=\rho\in\mathbb{Z}$, then the vector $(-\rho, r_1, ..., r_d)$ would contradict the hypothesis:
    \begin{equation*}
        -\rho+\sum_{i=1}^{d}=0
    \end{equation*}

    $\Leftarrow$ is actually the same thing, because you may notice that the steps of the last proof where all if and only ifs. So you may prove it by just changing the assumptions.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    $T_{\gamma}$ is topologically transitive $\iff$ the vector that defines it $(1,\gamma_1,..,\gamma_d)$ is rationally independent.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The proposition we will actually prove is the following: \begin{equation*}
        T_{\gamma} \text{topologically transitive }\iff \nexists (k_1,..,k_d)\neq \vec{0}\text{ such that }\sum_{i}k_i\gamma_i\in\mathbb{Z}
    \end{equation*}
    which, in light of the precedent propositions, is the exact same as the one we are trying to prove. 

    $\Rightarrow$ is proven by contradiction. RAA Let's assume that such a vector $k=(k_1,...,k_d)\neq0$, for which $\sum_ik_i\gamma_i=<k,\gamma>=p\in\mathbb{Z}$, actually exists. Then, we may take the following function: \begin{equation*}
        f(x)=e^{2i\pi<k, x>}
    \end{equation*}
    We must observe that said function is defined on the torus, because of Euler's formula. $f(x)$ is also continuous and non-constant. This means that if we are able to show that it is also T-invariant we have completed the proof, since $T_{\gamma}$ cannot be topologically transitive if there exists such a function. We shall start by observing that:
    \begin{equation*}
        f(T_{\gamma}(x))=e^{2i\pi<k, x+\gamma \mod \mathbb{Z}^d>}e^{2i\pi<k, x+\gamma>}
    \end{equation*}
    We are able to lose the $\mod \mathbb{Z}^d$ since it is akin as subtracting an integer to the exponent which, again because of Euler's formula, is the equivalent to multiplying the whole thing by 1.

    After having done that, we may substitute with $p\in\mathbb{Z}$ and use the same property: \begin{equation*}
        f(T_{\gamma}(x))=e^{2i\pi<k, x+\gamma>}=e^{2i\pi<k, x>}e^{2i\pi<k, \gamma>}=f(x)
    \end{equation*}
    So, said f(x) is also T-invariant, which is absurd.

    We must now prove $\Leftarrow$.

    RAA Since by hypothesis T is a homeomorphism, we are proving this by contradiction by using the lemma. We start by assuming that T is not topologically transitive, which implies there exist $U\neq\emptyset$ open, $\neq M$ and T-invariant\footnote{Actually, we are guaranteed two of those, but we will only need one.}.
    
    So, $f=1_U$ is T-invariant too, and it is not identically equal to $1_M$. At this point, we may also verify that $f\in L^2(T^d)$, L2 meaning square integrable: this is due to the fact that it is limited in a limited space. 
    
    We can then employ Fourier's representation: \begin{equation*}
    f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\hat{f}_ke^{2i\pi<k, x>}
    \end{equation*}
    If we do the same thing to $f\circ T$ we find:
    \begin{equation*}
        (f\circ T)(x)=f(T(x))=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\hat{f}_ke^{2i\pi<k, x+\gamma>}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\hat{f}_ke^{2i\pi<k, x>}e^{2i\pi<k, \gamma>}
    \end{equation*}
    Which means that $\hat{f}_ke^{2i\pi<k, \gamma>}$ is the Fourier basis of $f\circ T$. We must consider, though, that f has been established to be T-invariant (and Fourier is unique), so we should have $\hat{f}_ke^{2i\pi<k, \gamma>}=\hat{f}_k$. 

    This would be possible if $<k, \gamma>$ was, somehow, an integer, which is against the hypothesis. Another possibility is that $\hat{f}_k=0$. This, though, implies $f=\hat{f}_0$, a constant. Since f is an indicator function, this must mean that f is either 1 or 0, but both are impossible because they would imply a contradiction of the initial hypothesis: either $U=\emptyset$ or $U=M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    Suppose you have a manifold M of dimension d, with a semi-flow $(\phi^t)_{t\in\mathbb{R}^+}$ on it. Suppose you have a sub-manifold $M_0$ of dimension d-1 and assume that\begin{enumerate}
    \item For every x in M there exists a time $t^+(x)>0$ such that $\phi^{t^+}(x)\in M_0$, and that there exists some $t^{-}>0$ such that $\phi^{t-}(x)\cap M_0\neq \emptyset$ (consider that $\phi^{t-}(x)$ is a set because of the definition). Also, there exists some $\delta(x)$ such that no times with the above properties are in $[-\delta, \delta]\setminus\{0\}$ (in other words, times must not converge to 0). Set $t_{M_0}(x)=inf\{t>0|\phi^t(x)\in M_0\}\in(0,\infty)$: this is called hitting time to $M_0$, and if $x\in M_0$, return time. \footnote{Hw: realize that inf is a minimum};
    \item for every $U_0$ in $M_0$ open set in a topology of $M_0$ (induced by the topology of M), there exists some $\epsilon=\epsilon(U_0)>0$ such that $U=\cup_{0<t<\epsilon}\phi^{-t}U_0$ is open in M. This means that $U_0=A\cap M_0$ for some A open in M. (notice that we are defining being open in the cross-section);
    \item both $M$ and $M_0$ are "nice" spaces. 
\end{enumerate}

In this case, $M_0$ is called a Poincarè section for the semi-flow $(\phi^t)_{t>0}$ and $T:M_0\rightarrow M_0$, defined $T(x)=\phi^{t_{M_0}(x)}$, is called the corresponding Poincarè map or first return map.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    Consider a manifold M where the three conditions given by the Poincarè section definition hold.
    Then, for all (there exists(?)) $x_0\in M_0$ such that $\{\phi^t(x_0)\}_{t\geq 0}$ is dense in M $\iff$ $\{\phi^t(x_0)\}_{t\geq 0}=\{T^n_0(x_0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} \cap M_0$ is dense in $M_0$, where the notation refers to the entities defined in the Poincarè section definition.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}
    If $\phi^t$ is continuous, $t_{M_0(x)}$ is continuous as well and the three properties just mentioned hold, then \begin{equation*}
        (M, (\phi^t)_{t\geq0})\text{ is topologically }\underset{(minimal)}{transitive}\iff (M_0, T_0)\text{ is topologically }\underset{(minimal)}{transitive}
    \end{equation*}
    Where $T_0$ is the Poincarè map $T_0:M_0\rightarrow M_0, T_0=\phi^{t_{M_0}(x)}(x)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    First, it must be noticed that $T_0$ is continuous, since it's a composition of continuous functions by hypothesis.

    At this point, we may just use the lemma. If there is some $x_0\in M_0$ such that $\{\phi^t(x_0)\}_{t\geq 0}$, then obviously $(M_0, T_0)$ is topologically transitive (and the vice versa is true, so it's an if and only if). Because of the lemma, though, we can use the same logical process to prove that $(M, (\phi^t)_{t\geq 0})$ is topologically transitive, since we would know that $O_{\phi^t}(x)$ is dense in M for all $x\in\phi^{-s}(x_0)$ for some $s>0$, since $O_{\phi}(x_0)\subseteq O_{\phi}(x)$. Since everything we used is an if and only if, the proposition is proven.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Special dynamical system: the Kronecker flow on $T^d$}
The Kronecker flow on $T^d$ is the flow of the ODE \begin{equation*}
\dot{x}=\omega, \omega=(\omega_1,...,\omega_d)\in\mathbb{R}^d \end{equation*} which means that \begin{equation*}
\phi^t=\phi^t_{\omega}=(x+\omega t) \mod \mathbb{Z}^d=(x_1+tw_1, ..., x_d+tw_d) \mod 1
\end{equation*}
In this context, the following proposition is true.

\begin{proposition}
    $(T^d, \phi^t_{\omega})$ is minimal $\iff\omega_1, \omega_d$ are rationally independent
\end{proposition}
Without losing generality, we may say $\omega_d\neq 0$, since to be rationally independent you need at least one of the omegas to be different form 0, and in the same way such a flow cannot possibly be minimal if $\omega_i=\vec{0}$.

In 2d, it's quite easy to imagine a simple Poincarè section on the torus: the horizontal line. We are extending this concept to multiple dimensions: we consider $M_0=\{(x_1, x_{d-1}, 0)\in T^d\}\approxeq T^{d-1}$. Clearly, this is a Poincarè section for the flow, since $\omega_d\neq 0$, with $\forall x\in M_0, t_{M_0}(x)=1/\omega_d$. Now, we may define: \begin{equation*}
T_0(x)\approxeq T_0(x_1,..., x_d)=(x_1+\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_d},...,x_{d-1}+\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{\omega_d})\mod 1
\end{equation*}
which is a discrete translation in $T^{d-1}$. It is minimal if and only if $1, \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_d}, ...\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{\omega_d}$ are rationally independent, because of the proposition about translations of the torus. This is possible if and only if $\omega_1, ...\omega_d$ are rationally independent, which is what we were trying to prove.

    We define $T_2: T\rightarrow T$, $T_2(x)=2x \mod 1$, as shown \autoref{fig:doubling_map}.
    
    Thinking of the circle as $S^1=\{z\in\mathbb{C}| |z|=1\}$, then $T_2:S^1\rightarrow S^1, T_2(z)=z^2, T(e^{2i\pi\omega})=e^{4i\pi \omega}$. This is a non-invertible dynamical system, since it's subjective but not injective. The importance of this example stems from the fact that it's easy to see that even with such a simple map an increasing divergence from the starting conditions takes place. The translation did not have that, neither did the Kronecker flow. 
    
    Another reason for which this is particularly interesting is the fact that this map has to do with the binary representation of numbers.
    
    Say we are trying to represent $x\in[0,1)$ as a binary sequence of digits. So, \begin{equation}
    x=0.d_1d_2..._2,\, d_i\in\{0,1\}
    \label{eq:binary}
    \end{equation}
    where the numbers are determined by the equation
    \begin{equation}
        x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_i2^{-i}
    \label{eq:decimal}
    \end{equation}
    Notice that the binary representation is unique, while the decimal one is not. In fact, \begin{equation*}
    x=\sum_{i\geq 1}d_i2^i\leftrightarrow (d_i)_{i\geq1}
    \end{equation*}
    is almost bijective, due to the fact that the binary representation is not unique.
    
    To see it better, imagine the numbers $0.0\bar{1}_2$ and $0.1_2$ in the sense of the binary representation in\autoref{eq:binary}. Those are computed in the following ways: \begin{align*}
        0.1_2=\frac{1}{2} \\
        0.0\bar{1}_2=\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}2^{-i}=\frac{1}{2}
    \end{align*}
    so, we are representing the same number with two different binary representation.
    
    You can ignore this problem it in two possible ways: notice that the problem is only for some points $0.d_1....0\bar{1}=0.d_1...1\bar{0}$, which are a countable set. In short, this comparison fails in countably many points, which we can simply ignore, as they are countably many (i.e. not many). We may alsp make it bijective by always choosing one of the two possibilities at ambiguous points.

    In this context, \begin{equation*}
        T_2(x)=T_(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_i2^{-i})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_i2^{-i+1}\mod 1=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{j+1}2^{-j}\mod 1=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}d_{j+1}2^{-j}
    \end{equation*}
    where we got rid of the mod 1 because we never go over 1.

\begin{definition}
    A topological dynamical system $(M, \phi^t)$ is called topologically mixing if for all $U, V$ non-empty open sets there exists an $N=N(U, V)\geq 0$ such that for all $t\geq N, \phi^t(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset$
\end{definition}

Trivially, topological mixing implies topological transitivity, so it's a bit stronger as a definition.

\begin{proposition}
    $T_2$ is topologically mixing.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $U, V$ be two arbitrary set. There exist $N, k \in\mathbb{N}$ such that $[\frac{k}{2^N}, \frac{k+1}{2^N})\subset U$.
    
    Notice that $T^n_2(x)=2^nx\mod 1$\footnote{Convince yourself via this example: \begin{equation*}
    (2x \mod 1)^2=2^2x \mod 1 
    \end{equation*}}.

    This implies that, for $n>N$, $T^n_2([\frac{k}{2^N}, \frac{k+1}{2^N}))=[0,1)$ because of the mod 1.
    
    This includes any V, even if we started from a subset of U.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Special class of maps: hyperbolic automorphism of the torus}
\begin{definition}
    Hyperbolic automorphisms of the torus are maps such that \begin{equation*}
    T_A:T^d\rightarrow T^d,\, T_A(x)=Ax \mod \mathbb{Z}^d
    \end{equation*}
    where $A\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d},\, \det A=1$\footnote{For those that like linear algebra, that is the same as saying $A\in SL_d(\mathbb{Z})$}.
\end{definition}

    Let's now study A. Its eigenvalues are \begin{equation*}
    \lambda_1=\lambda=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\text{ and }\lambda_2=\lambda^{-1}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
    \end{equation*}
    The eigenvectors, on the other hand, are \begin{equation*}
    v_1=(1, \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2})\text{ and }v_2=(1, -\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2})
    \end{equation*}
    They are usually called $v^u$ and $v^s$, and they are orthogonal, i.e. $<v^u, v^s>=0$, since A is symmetric. Let's look at periodic points.

    Let's start from $\Leftarrow$.
    
    Let $x\in\mathbb{Q}^2$ be written as $x=(\frac{p_1}{q}, \frac{p_2}{q})$. This is obviously possible since you can always find a common denominator, and they do not have to be reduced to the lowest terms. We may then notice that \begin{equation*}
        x=\left(\frac{p_1}{q}, \frac{p_2}{q}\right)=\frac{1}{q}(p_1, p_2)
    \end{equation*}
    which, combined with the fact that $T_A$ is invertible, implies that the orbit is the following: \begin{equation*}
        O(x)=\left\{\frac{1}{q}A^n(p_1, p_2) \mod \mathbb{Z}^2\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
    \end{equation*}
    We notice that $A^n(p_1, p_2)\in\mathbb{Z}^2$, and that, as shown in \autoref{fig:cat_rational_periodic}, implies the following:
    \begin{equation*}
        O(x)=\left\{\frac{1}{q}A^n(p_1, p_2) \mod \mathbb{Z}^2\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq \frac{\mathbb{Z}^2}{q}\cap T^2
    \end{equation*}
    This makes the whole thing periodic, since because $(\mathbb{Z}^2/q)\cap T^2$ has a finite amount of points, so at one point you are certainly going to get to a point you already visited\footnote{This is called the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigeonhole_principle}{pigeonhole principle.}}. 
    
    At this point, we know that there is some periodic point. To complete the proof, we must show if this point is x. This is implied by the fact that $A$ is invertible\footnote{Observe $A^{-1}\in SL_d(\mathbb{Z})$, i.e. $A^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times2}, \det A=1$, $A=\begin{bmatrix}
        a & b \\ c & d
    \end{bmatrix}$ then $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\begin{bmatrix}
        d & -b \\ -c & a
    \end{bmatrix}$ which are both in SL since the determinant is 1. 

    HW $T_A\circ T_{A^{-1}}=T_{A^{-1}}\circ T_{A}=\mathbb{I}$ on $T^2$. }.  In fact, imagine that the periodic point was not the starting point: if that was the case, there would be such a point for which you could not go back in time surely, since (referring to the visualization as in the image) it would have two arrows pointing to it, i.e. two possible pasts.

    Let's now prove $\Rightarrow$. 

    The hypothesis that x is periodic implies, by definition, that there exists some n for which $T_A^n(x)$, which can be reworked as \begin{equation*}
    T_A^n(x)=x\iff A^nx=x \mod 1\iff(A^n-I)x\in\mathbb{Z}^2\iff x=(A^n-I)^{-1}k
    \end{equation*} for some k in $\mathbb{Z}^2$. 
    
    We know that the spectrum of such a matrix to be $\{\lambda^n-1, \lambda^{-n}-1\}$, so it does not contain 0. Using that, by knowing how to compute the inverse of a matrix (refer to note 15), we may end the proof: \begin{equation*}A^n-I\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times2}\Rightarrow(A^n-I)^{-1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{2\times2}\Rightarrow x\in\mathbb{Q}^2 \cap T \end{equation*}
    Where, in the last passage, we used the facts that $(A^n-I)x\in\mathbb{Z}^2$ and that we know, by hypothesis, that $x\in T$.
\end{proof}
\begin{observation}
    Two corollaries of this proposition are that $Per(T_A)$ is dense in $T^2$ and that $T_A$ is not minimal.
\end{observation}

\begin{definition}
    Given $x=(x_1, x_2)\in T^2$, we define the unstable manifold as \begin{equation*}
    W^u(x)\{y=(y_1, y_2)\in T^2| y_2-x_2=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}(y_1-x_1) \mod 1\}
    \end{equation*}
    while we define the stable manifold as \begin{equation*}
        W^s(x)\{y=(y_1, y_2)\in T^2| y_2-x_2=-\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}(y_1-x_1) \mod 1\}
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

Those are called stable and unstable because they are the curves with the property that, when taking two points on the unstable one, they get further away from each other, while when picking two on the stable one results in the opposite outcome, as we saw in linear maps with fixed points(although here there are no fixed points). Notice that stable and unstable manifolds' concepts are universal in the study of dynamical systems, although we defined them in the non-general framework of the cat map.

This can be proven more rigorously.

\begin{proposition}
    Let $y\in \underset{W^{s}(x)}{W^{u}(x)}$. Then, considering $d()$ as the distance on the torus: \begin{equation*}
    d(T_A^n(y), T_A^n(x))\leq C\lambda^{-|n|}
    \end{equation*} for $n\underset{\geq}{\leq} 0$ and some constant $C(x, y)$. 
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We will only prove the unstable one.

        A^n(y-x)=(\lambda^{-1})^{|n|}y-x\Rightarrow||A^n(y-x)||_{\mathbb{R}^2}=(\lambda^{-1})^{|n|}||y-x||_{\mathbb{R}^2}
    \end{equation*}
    because $y-x$ is a multiple of $v^u$ (remember that n is either negative or 0). 
    
    But $d(T_A^n(y), T_A^n(x))\leq ||A^ny-A^nx||_{\mathbb{R}^2}$, with $n=-|n|$, because the mod 1 either makes the points either be equally distanced or closer (intuitively: the distance on the torus never makes any $\mathbb{R}^2$ distance bigger, only smaller or the same.).

        d(T_A^n(y), T_A^n(x))\leq ||A^ny-A^nx||_{\mathbb{R}^2}=(\lambda^{-1})^{|n|}||y-x||_{\mathbb{R}^2}
    \end{equation*}
    And $||y-x||$ is the constant C\footnote{You could actually prove that $||y-x||\leq\sqrt{2}/2$ for any couple of points, thus proving a stronger claim than the proposition}.
\end{proof}

One application of this are Lagrangian coherent structures. Take for example weather forecast: you will see clouds spreading in one direction and shrinking in the other one. The machine that's doing the prediction is attempting to make an informed guess over the stable and unstable directions of the clouds.

A corollary of this proposition is the following:
\begin{proposition}
    If $f: T^2\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is continuous, then \begin{equation*}
        \forall y \in \underset{W^{s}(x)}{W^{u}(x)},\,|f(T_A^n(y))-f(T_A^n(x))|\underset{n\rightarrow\underset{+\infty}{-\infty}}{\longrightarrow} 0
    \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    $T^2$ is compact, which implies that f, being continuous, is actually uniformly continuous. This means that for every $\epsilon$ there is a $\delta$ for which, if $|y-x|\leq\epsilon$, then $|f(y)-f(x)|\leq \delta$. In layman terms, if you apply the function to a couple of points, they get as close as you want as the points get closer.

    We will conclude the proof just in the stable case, meaning that $n\rightarrow+\infty$. In light of the fact that f is uniformly continuous:\begin{equation*}
        |T_A^n(y)-T_A^n(x)|\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow} 0\Rightarrow|f(T_A^n(y))-f(T_A^n(x))|\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow} 0
    \end{equation*}
    Since we have just proven that the first assertion is true, then the second one must also be true.
\end{proof}

    Let U, V be as in the definition of topologically mixing. This means U is non-empty, so there is some $\bar{x}\in U$. We then define $J=U\cap W^u(\bar{x})$.  As for V, we choose an $\epsilon$ small enough that V contains a disk of radius $\epsilon$\footnote{Here I call it a disk, but a more mathematical-minded person might call this a 2D open ball of radius $\epsilon$.}; this is guaranteed to be doable by the fact that V is a non-empty open set.

    Then, we define $J'=J-\bar{x}$, which implies $J'\subseteq W^u(0)$, as we have already seen. So, there exists $\bar{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^+$ such that, for every $n\geq\bar{n},\,T^nJ'$ is $\epsilon$-dense, since $T^nJ'$ gets longer and longer; in fact, the slope of $W^u(0)$ is irrational, so, by applying what we know about the Kronecker flow on $T^2$ with direction $w=(1, \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2})$, we can easily see the $\epsilon$-density. \footnote{I am uncertain about this part. I feel like it's not the kronecker flow. Nonetheless, he did say kronecker flow. Also, I probably erroneously called dense something that was $\epsilon$-dense before in this chapter, which is why I cannot find what he was referring to. At the same time, the density is heuristically pretty direct, so you should be able to understand the result.}

    If $T^n_A(J')$ is $\epsilon$-dense, then the same is true for J, because it's a translation, meaning $T^n_A(J)=T^n_A(J')+T^n_A(\bar{x})$. This means that $T^n_A(J)\cap V\neq\emptyset$. But \begin{equation*}
        T^n_A(J)\subseteq T^n_A(U)\Rightarrow T^n_A(U) \cap V\neq \emptyset
    \end{equation*}
    which is exactly the definition of topologically mixing.
\end{proof}

Then, take an arbitrary rectangle in the plane, and an arbitrary point in said rectangle (again, by using the knobs). Now: say that our space has a contracting and an expanding direction, respectively the vertical and horizontal ones. Then, the rectangle that we took before must stretch in the horizontal direction while shrinking in the vertical one, as time passes. Now, try to visualize that would happen if we could see the horizontal and vertical lines we use to select our starting point in the new, stretched rectangle: if we move our initial point vertically, thus translating the horizontal line, at one point the original horizontal line and the one in the future will inevitably be the same line, and the same is true for the horizontal one. The points for which this happens are the periodic points. If we are dealing with a torus, this rectangle will wrap around the torus, and so the periodic points will be more and more. The way the future rectangle behaved is an index of chaoticity, after all: high sensitivity to the initial conditions. In short, we just showed, heuristically, how a high number of periodic points is the result of high chaoticity; the whole reasoning is shown in \autoref{fig:periodic_points_thought}.

\begin{definition}[R. Devaney's definition of chaos (1986)]
    For a dynamical system on a metric space we say that $(M, \phi^t)$ is Devaney chaotic if\begin{enumerate}
        \item $(M, \phi^t)$ is topologically transitive;
        \item $Per(\phi^t)$ is dense in M;
        \item there is sensitive dependence on the initial conditions, i.e. \begin{equation*}
            \exists \delta>0 | \forall x\in M, \forall U \text{ nbhd of } x \exists x'\in U, t\in\mathbb{R}^+(\mathbb{Z}^+) | d(\phi^t(x'), \phi^t(x))>\delta
        \end{equation*}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Let's look at some of the systems we dealt with.

\begin{proposition}
    $T_2$ is Devaney chaotic.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    First, remember the way the graph of $T_2$ looks both on the torus and on the circle, as shown in \autoref{fig:doubling_map}; as such, it is also easy to recall that it is continuous. We have already proven that it is topologically transitive and that the periodic points are dense (we actually know more than that, they are the dyadic numbers). 

    So, we just need to prove the third assertion in the definition; the simplest way to do so is by simply realizing that by taking two arbitrary points, their distance doubles every time step. Noticing that, let's take any $0<\delta\leq0.25$, and also any $x$ and $U$. Now, let's also take some $x'\neq x$ in such a set; there are two situations possible: either $d(x, x')\geq\delta$, either $0<d(x, x')<\delta$. In the first case, we already meet the definition either for $t=0$ or $t=1$. 

    In the second case, it is certain that there is some even $k$ for which $\frac{\delta}{k}<d(x, x')$. So, you can halve $\delta$ enough to get a number smaller than any distance. This directly implies that if $t>k$, then $d(T_2^t(x), T_2^t(x'))>\delta$; notice that this is guaranteed by the boundaries we took from $\delta$, since on $T^1$ the maximum distance is 0.5.
\end{proof}

\textbf{HW} Show that $T_A$ (Arnold's cat map) is Devaney chaotic.

We have manually shown some maps to be Devaney chaotic, but in reality, under very broad conditions, you can prove that topological transitivity and density of periodic points implies sensitive dependence on initial conditions. To show that we need a lemma.

\begin{lemma}
    Let $(M, T^t)$ be a dynamical system on a metric space, then: \begin{align*}
        (M, T^t)\text{ is top. transitive, }Per(T^t)\text{ is dense, }T\in C^1,\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\Rightarrow \\
        \exists\bar{\delta}>0| \forall x\in M, \exists q=q(x)\in Per(T)|dist(O(q),x)>\bar{\delta}
    \end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Due to the density of periodic points, we can certainly state that there are at least two periodic points $q_1\neq q_2 \in Per(T)$ such that $O(q_1)\cap O(q_2)=\emptyset$.
    
    This directly implies that there is some distance between those orbits, which we call $3\bar{\delta}$: \begin{equation*}
    3\bar{\delta}=dist(O(q_1), O(q_2))>0
    \end{equation*}

    Now, if I take a random point $x$ there are two possible situations: either $dist(x, O(q_1))> \bar{\delta}$, in which case $q_1$ is the $q(x)$ of the proposition, or $dist(x, O(q_1))\leq \bar{\delta}$; in that case: \begin{equation*}
    dist(x, O(q_2))\geq \bar{\delta}
    \end{equation*} 
    This has to be true because otherwise, by triangular equality, \begin{equation*}
        dist(O(q_1), O(q_2))\leq dist(O(q_1), x)+dist(x, O(q_2))\leq 2\bar{\delta}
    \end{equation*}
    which is a contradiction. So, $dist(x, O(q(x)))>\bar{\delta}$, where $q_2=q(x)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[1992]
    Let $(M, T^t)$ be a dynamical system in a metric space. Then, if $T$ is continuous and $t\in\mathbb{Z}$: \begin{equation*}
        (1.)+(2.)\Rightarrow(3.)
    \end{equation*}
    where (1.), (2.) and (3.) are the conditions in the definition of Devaney's chaoticity.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let's start by setting $\delta=\frac{\bar{\delta}}{4}$ where $\bar{\delta}$ is the quantity that we are guaranteed to exist by the lemma, which clearly applies in this situation. Then, given $x, U$ as in (3.), set $U_0=U\cap B_{\delta}(x)$, which is open since it is the intersection of two open sets. Because of the density of periodic points, there must be some periodic point $p\in U_0\cap Per(T)$. Let's call its period $m$. Also, we will call $q=q(x)$ the periodic point guaranteed to exist by the lemma.

    Let's also set \begin{equation*}
    V=\cap^{m-1}_{i=0} T^{-i} (B_{\delta}(T^i(q)))
    \end{equation*}
    We may observe that $V$ is open due to the continuity of T and non-empty because $q\in V$. Basically, as shown in the image, $V$ is the set such that if you take a point in it, you will always be as close as $\delta$ to the trajectory of q.

    Another consideration that we must make is that, because of topological transitivity, you can have some point $y\in U_0, k\in\mathbb{Z}^+$, such that $T^{k}(y)\in V$ (remember: topological transitivity was about two arbitrary open sets). Also, we may set $j=\lceil\frac{k}{m}\rceil$\footnote{This is the ceiling function, which is defined as \begin{equation*} \lceil x \rceil=\min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}|n\geq x\}\end{equation*}}. For such a j, we can say:\begin{equation*}j-1< \frac{k}{m} \leq j\Rightarrow mj\footnote{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8ZZR3HIDFe0}-m<k\leq mj\footnote{This is note number 23.} \Leftrightarrow 0\leq mj-k <m\end{equation*}
    So, $mj-k$ is a time between the beginning and the end of a period.

    Now, let's take into consideration $T^{mj}(p)$ and $T^{mj}(y)$. Since p is by definition a periodic point,  $T^{mj}(p)=p$, while for the other one we may say: \begin{equation*}
    T^{mj}(y)=T^{mj-k}(T^k(y))\in T^{mj-k}V
    \end{equation*}
    where the last inclusion is true by definition of $y$ and $k$.

    Finally, $T^{mj}(p)$ is $\delta$-close to x, because $p$ is in $U_0$; also, $T^{mj}(y)$ is $\delta$-close to O(q) because of the definition of $V$ (remember, y is a point that after k time steps is in V). So, by definition, we may say: \begin{equation*}
    dist(x, O(q))>\bar{\delta}=4\delta\Rightarrow dist(T^{mj}(p), T^{mj}(y))>2\delta
    \end{equation*}
    And the proof is complete, since we are in the same situation as in the proof of the lemma: it cannot be that $T^{mj}(x)$ is $\delta$-close to both $T^{mj}(p)$ and $T^{mj}(y)$. Since we are trying to prove that there is some $x'$ such that, for some time ($mj$ in this case), the distance between $T^t(x)$ and $T^t(x')$ is more than $\delta$, the theorem is proved by taking $x'$ to be either $p$ or $y$, depending on which one is further away, as in the lemma.
\end{proof}
